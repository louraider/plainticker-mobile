**Where they agree, which is the settled part of the round.** Both critics killed the split
wordmark, both declared the tracking-gauge sentence dead, and neither disputed a single
measurement. The split wordmark is the one I brought into the round believing in, and Codex did not
kill it on taste, it found the defect in the argument: the design system's rule is that *ticker
symbols* and numerals are set in the monospace face, and "Ticker" in the product's name is an
English word, not a ticker symbol. The setting claimed to apply a rule it did not apply. That is
not a matter of preference and there is nothing to weigh against it. The whole name is set in
Outfit.

**Where they disagree, first: which mark.** Antigravity ranks short-measure first and says it
"genuinely answers Codex's objection ... it explicitly proves that the foundation cannot justify
the weight of a printed figure." Codex, which wrote that objection, says it does not: "the product
refuses to print an unreliable figure, while this mark visibly prints a small figure. The empty
area above it is ordinary chart background, not an observable act of withholding."

I follow Codex, and not because it owns the sentence. Its reason is checkable against the product
rather than against a taste. Below $10,000 of pool the app prints nothing at all; it does not print
a small number. A mark that draws a short bar in the third place depicts a behaviour this app does
not have, and it depicts it in the one place the app is unlike every neighbour it will sit beside.
Antigravity's reading requires the viewer to know that the stub measures support while the two tall
blocks measure readings, and nothing in the drawing says so: same baseline, same vocabulary, same
kind of quantity. Short-measure is out.

**Where they disagree, second: the ground.** Antigravity says keep the Canvas near-black: "if the
Canvas ground bleeds into a dark drawer and loses the superellipse mask, that borderless darkness
is a truer expression of the product than a bright, safe paper wrapper." Codex says ship paper for
visibility and stop treating a contrast ratio as evidence of distinctiveness.

I follow Codex. Antigravity's answer is an aesthetic preference set against a measurement, and the
measurement is the one thing three rounds of this have produced: 1.03 to 1 is not a dark tile, it
is no tile, and attempt three's compositor could not locate PlainTicker's own slot in the
screenshot by scanning because there was nothing there to find. A brand argument whose conclusion
is that the icon should be invisible in the drawer is not a brand argument. Codex's caveat is
adopted rather than argued with, and it corrects my own wording: on paper the mark does not keep
Ink and Accent literally, the strokes become dark ink, so what survives is the two-colour
structure, and 16.89 to 1 buys a boundary and not distinctiveness. Distinctiveness has to come from
the drawing.

**The change that mattered was not either critic's asked-for change.** It was one sentence of
Codex's reasoning: kept-place "does not visibly reserve a third place. There is no repeated
container, divider or other structure making that empty region a slot." That is correct about the
drawing it saw, where the row was one continuous rule with a blue end, and it is the difference
between a mark that means something and a mark with a caption. The row is now three separate
registers on the same pitch as the places above them: two carry a figure and the third is drawn,
registered and empty. A battery has one container. No chart draws the register of a value it does
not have.

**Both asked-for changes are drawn and neither is adopted as stated.**

Antigravity asked for the Accent stub at 4 units instead of 8. It is drawn as `short-measure-agy`,
rendered at every size and measured in the drawer. Four units is 3.4dp and 10 device pixels on the
Seeker tile, under the 6-unit floor this repository has enforced since attempt one, whose 2-unit
tick was 1.3dp and was simply not present on the phone. Halving the only element that carries the
meaning is attempt one with a different number on it, and it is applied to a drawing that lost on
other grounds anyway.

Codex asked for the mark at exactly 1.00 of the wordmark's cap height in the horizontal lockup. It
is drawn, and it was aimed at a mark that no longer exists: the "pause-and-tray" shape it was
correcting is the continuous row that Codex's own paragraph five got rid of. What the instruction
encodes, that the mark had more authority than its meaning earns, is taken, but by a rule rather
than by a number: the mark's figure blocks now stand at exactly the wordmark's cap height and its
registers hang below the wordmark's baseline the way a descender does, which puts the mark 1.294
cap heights tall overall. Codex can still say that is 29 percent too much. Both drawings are on
this page and the founder can see the argument rather than read it.

**The claim in .agents/product-marketing.md does not survive.** "The brand mark is the tracking
gauge itself rather than a letter, because it is the one mark nobody else has and it says what the
app does." Both critics rejected it independently, having seen the gauge inverted and given the
edge it never had. Codex: "There is no reference, range or relationship that identifies tracking
... 'nobody else has' is an unsupported exclusivity claim, and 'says what the app does' assigns the
drawing information it does not contain." Antigravity: "the marketing copy is defending a ghost."

The deeper reason, which neither critic needed and which decides it anyway: the gauge is drawn only
above the liquidity floor. Below $10,000 of pool the app does not draw it. A brand mark cannot be
the one element the product withholds; it has to be the thing that is true of every screen. What is
always true is the withholding itself. Both critics offered a replacement sentence and Antigravity's
is the better one, so it is the one proposed:

> The brand mark is the refusal itself: the deliberate empty space left behind when a pool is too
> thin to track.
